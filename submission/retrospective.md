# FlyRank AI Fluency — Final Retrospective

When I started the FlyRank AI Fluency track, I wanted to become more comfortable using AI as a practical tool rather than only as something that could answer questions or generate code. My goal was to learn how to take an idea, work with real data, build something useful, evaluate the result, and communicate it clearly.

The project changed significantly as I worked through the weeks. What initially looked like a machine-learning task became a broader engineering and problem-solving exercise. I worked with search-intelligence data containing tens of millions of records and had to think carefully about what one row represented, which signals were meaningful, how the target should be defined, and how the data could be processed efficiently. The scale of the dataset also made me realize that working with real-world data is very different from working with small datasets used in tutorials.

One of the most important decisions I made was to establish a simple baseline before relying on a more sophisticated machine-learning approach. The baseline used signals such as content staleness and impression decline to produce an action score and categorize content into actions such as `REFRESH_NOW`, `REVIEW`, and `MONITOR`. This gave me an interpretable starting point and made the later ML evaluation more meaningful. Instead of simply asking whether a model produced a good score, I could ask whether the added complexity of machine learning actually improved on a simpler approach.

A major part of the experience was debugging. Data loading, authentication, large parquet datasets, notebook execution, and feature preparation all created problems that I had to work through. These problems taught me that successful ML work is not just about writing a model-training function. Understanding errors, checking assumptions, validating outputs, and making sure that the pipeline actually runs are equally important.

AI played a significant role in my workflow. I used AI assistants to explain unfamiliar concepts, suggest debugging approaches, help structure code, review ideas, and improve documentation. However, I learned not to assume that an AI-generated answer was automatically correct. I had to run the code, inspect the results, investigate errors, and make decisions myself. That changed the way I think about AI: it is more useful as a development partner and reasoning assistant than as a replacement for verification.

The biggest change in how I work is that I now think more deliberately about the complete workflow. I am less likely to jump immediately into coding. I first ask what problem I am solving, what the unit of analysis is, what the target means, what baseline I can establish, and how I will know whether the final result is actually better. I also understand the importance of documenting limitations instead of presenting a model as if it were perfect.

There are still several things I would improve. I would like to evaluate the model across additional time periods and different segments of the data to understand how well it generalizes. I would also like to connect the model's predictions more directly to business outcomes and eventually build a more complete system that could monitor content performance continuously and provide useful recommendations to content teams.

Three lessons from this project are especially transferable to my future work. First, **define the problem before building the solution**; a technically impressive model is not useful if the target and success criteria are unclear. Second, **build a baseline before adding complexity**; a simple benchmark provides context for every improvement that follows. Third, **treat evaluation, debugging, and communication as part of engineering**, not as tasks that happen after the real work is finished.

Looking back at Week 1, the biggest difference is that I no longer see AI simply as a tool for getting answers. I see it as a partner that can help me explore, build, debug, evaluate, and communicate — while still requiring me to verify the work and take responsibility for the final result.
